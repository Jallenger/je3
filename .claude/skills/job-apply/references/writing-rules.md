# Writing rules

The goal is a document that reads as though a competent person wrote it about
their own career. Two failure modes to avoid: sounding machine-written, and
looking machine-made.

## Words and phrases that give it away

Never use: spearheaded, leveraged, proven track record, results-driven,
results-oriented, dynamic, passionate, seamless, seamlessly, robust, holistic,
synergy, drive value, deliver value, value-add, best-in-class, cutting-edge,
world-class, fast-paced environment, wear many hats, hit the ground running,
game-changer, transformative journey, empowered, championed, orchestrated,
navigated, honed, delve, tapestry, testament to, underscores, pivotal, myriad.

Also avoid the softening qualifiers that pile up in generated prose:
"helping to", "working to", "instrumental in", "played a key role in",
"contributed to". Either the person did the thing or they did not. If they
shared it, name who with.

## Punctuation and rhythm

- **No em dashes.** The single strongest tell. Use a full stop, a comma, or
  restructure. En dashes in date ranges are correct and expected.
- No "not just X, but Y" constructions. No "It's not about X. It's about Y."
- Break the parallelism. Generated bullets are all the same length and all
  start with a past-tense verb. Vary it: start some with the object, some with
  a time or context clause, let one be a short flat sentence after three long
  ones.
- Avoid tricolons. Three-item lists everywhere is a rhythm, and a recognisable
  one. Use two items, or four, or a clause instead.
- Do not end bullets on an abstract benefit clause ("...driving efficiency
  across the organisation"). End on the fact.

## Substance

- Numbers beat adjectives. "Fewer than five people resigned across that period"
  is worth more than "industry-leading retention".
- Specifics that only this person could write are what make it read as human:
  the actual thing built, the actual client situation, the constraint that made
  it hard.
- Say what was hard or contested where it is true and creditable. Generated
  resumes are uniformly triumphant, which is itself a tell.
- One voice. First person implied, no "I" at the start of every bullet, but the
  profile paragraph can use "I" and should — it reads as a person talking.

## Australian conventions

Australian English: organisation, specialised, recognised, prioritise,
utilise (prefer plain "use"), centre, licence (noun) / license (verb),
fulfilment, programme only for a scheme, otherwise program.

Dates as `Jul 2023 – Aug 2026`. Phone as `0435 018 197`. Money as `$1M`,
`$10M`. State abbreviations after the city: `Canberra, ACT`.

## Look

- Serif body (Cambria, Garamond) reads more considered than default Calibri,
  which is what an untouched Word document looks like.
- No icons, no emoji, no photographs, no logos.
- No skill bars, rating dots, star ratings, or percentage-proficiency graphics.
  These say "template" instantly and carry no information.
- No colour blocks or sidebars. One restrained ink colour and one rule colour.
- Two pages for an executive resume. One page is junior; three is unread.
- Section headings in small caps or letterspaced caps with a hairline rule
  under them. Dates right-aligned on a positional tab, never padded with spaces
  or dot leaders.
- Keep it parseable by applicant tracking systems: real text, no text boxes, no
  tables carrying the main career content. A two-column label grid for skills
  is fine; a two-column page layout is not.
